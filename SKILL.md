# LinkedIn Carousel Creator

Turn whatever topic the user brings you into a finished LinkedIn carousel they can build in Canva (or any slide tool). The output of this skill is **slide content about the user's topic** — headlines and body copy, slide by slide, plus a caption — written in their voice and grounded in their real expertise, stories, and examples.

## Core Principle (read first)

**The deliverable is a carousel about the USER'S topic — not a carousel about how carousels work.**

- If the user says "make me a carousel about cold-water swimming," you produce slides about cold-water swimming, not slides about LinkedIn engagement tactics.
- The best-practice research in this skill (slide counts, dwell time, save rates, timing, dimensions, etc.) is **invisible craft knowledge**. Use it to shape *how* you write the slides — tight copy, a sharp hook, one idea per slide, the right length. **Do not quote these statistics back to the user, and do not pad the carousel with them.** Surface a stat only if the user explicitly asks "why" or asks for the data.
- A carousel stuffed with engagement benchmarks instead of substance on the user's topic is a **failure**, even if every number is correct.

## Step 1: Discovery Interview (do this before writing anything)

A carousel is only as good as how well it fits the person posting it. Unless the user has already given you all of this, **ask a focused round of questions first** — then write. Group them so the user can answer quickly; don't interrogate one at a time.

Ask about:

1. **The topic & core takeaway** — What's the subject, and what's the single most important thing you want a reader to walk away knowing or believing?
2. **You & your credibility** — What's your relationship to this topic? What have you personally done, built, or learned here that makes this *yours* and not generic advice?
3. **Specific material** — Do you have concrete examples, numbers, a personal story, a mistake you made, a result you got, or a hot take? (This is what makes the carousel worth saving — push for specifics here.)
4. **Audience** — Who is this for (role, industry, experience level)? What do they already believe or struggle with?
5. **Voice & tone** — How do you want to sound: punchy and contrarian, warm and personal, analytical and precise, playful? Any words/phrases you do or don't use? (If unsure, ask for a link or sample of how they normally write.)
6. **Goal & desired action** — What should this post *do* for you (build authority, get DMs, drive signups, spark debate), and what should a reader do at the end (comment, save, follow, click)?

If the user is in a hurry or gives a thin brief, make reasonable assumptions, **state them explicitly**, draft anyway, and invite correction — don't block on a full interview. But never skip straight to a generic deck when one or two questions would make it genuinely theirs.

Once you have enough, pick the narrative arc and content type that fit (Part 1) and write the slides.

## Part 1: Strategy & Planning

### Define Your Hook

Your first slide determines whether people swipe or scroll. It must:
- Lead with a bold claim, surprising statistic, or compelling question
- State the value proposition clearly (what will they gain?)
- Use 10 words or fewer for body text
- Include a "swipe" indicator (arrow, "→", or "Swipe for...")

**Hook formulas that work:**
- Challenge: "Why [common belief] is costing you [outcome]"
- Question: "How do you [solve problem without obvious solution]?"
- Promise: "The [specific thing] framework I use to [result]"
- Stat: "[Number]% of teams miss this [important practice]"

### Choose Your Narrative Arc

Select one structure to organize your slides:

**Problem → Insight → Example → Action** (4 slides minimum)
- Slide 1: Hook with problem statement
- Slide 2: Why it matters / counterintuitive insight
- Slide 3: Concrete example or case study
- Slide 4+: Actionable steps or framework
- Final: CTA

**Numbered Framework** (5-7 slides)
- Slide 1: Hook introducing the framework
- Slides 2-6: One step/principle per slide (e.g., "The 5-C Framework")
- Final: How to apply or save for later

**Step-by-Step Process** (6-8 slides)
- Slide 1: Hook with expected outcome
- Slides 2-7: One action per slide with expected result
- Final: Result or next milestone

**Before/After or Progression** (5-6 slides)
- Slide 1: Hook + the transformation
- Slides 2-4: "Before" state (problems, challenges)
- Slide 5: Pivot or insight moment
- Slide 6+: "After" state or transformation

**Personal Narrative** (6-8 slides)
- Slide 1: Hook (surprising outcome or lesson)
- Slides 2-5: Story progression
- Slides 6-7: The insight / lesson learned
- Final: How it applies to viewers

## Part 2: Content Development

### Slide-by-Slide Outline

Use this template. Write only headlines at first; add body text in a second pass.

```
SLIDE 1 (HOOK)
Headline: [10 words max]
Body: [Optional: 1-2 lines clarifying value]
Visual note: Bold, high contrast, one focal point

SLIDE 2
Headline: [One idea per slide]
Body: [2-4 lines, aim for 15-30 words]
Visual note:

SLIDE 3
Headline:
Body:
Visual note:

[Continue for each slide...]

FINAL SLIDE (CTA)
Headline: [Save this? Comment? Follow?]
Body: [One clear action]
Visual note: Contrasting background, large type
```

### Content Rules to Avoid Dead Carousels

**The 5-Second Rule**: If a slide needs more than 5 seconds to read, users will scroll past.
- Limit to 2-4 lines of body text per slide
- Maximum 30 words of explanatory text
- Headlines should be scannable in 2 seconds

**One Idea Per Slide**: If you're tempted to put two columns or two concepts on one slide, split it into two slides instead.

**Use Numbering**: "1. First idea" "2. Second idea" creates forward momentum and improves dwell time by 20-30%.

**Headline-First Design**: Write compelling headlines first. Many high-performing carousels use headline-only slides with minimal body text.

**Avoid Engagement Bait**: "Swipe to see ___" without context doesn't work. Your hook must deliver value upfront, not hide it.

## Part 3: Design Specifications

### Technical Requirements

**File Format & Size**
- Format: PDF (not PowerPoint or images)
- File size: Doesn't matter (LinkedIn accepts up to 100MB)
- Pages: Each PDF page becomes one swipeable slide

**Dimensions** (Choose ONE)
- **Portrait (Recommended)**: 1080 × 1350 px (4:5 aspect ratio)
  - Takes most vertical space on mobile feeds
  - Best for 60%+ mobile-first audiences
- **Square**: 1080 × 1080 px (1:1 aspect ratio)
  - Works well on desktop and mobile
  - Balanced option
- **Landscape**: 1920 × 1080 px (16:9)
  - Not recommended; wastes horizontal space on mobile

**Export Settings**
- Format: PDF Standard (not PDF Print)
- Compression: Medium (keeps text crisp while staying lightweight)
- Embed fonts: Yes

### Design Principles

**Consistency Across All Slides**
- Use the same 2 fonts max throughout
- Stick to 3 brand colors maximum
- Keep layout grid consistent (same margins, same text areas)
- Logo placement in same spot on every slide
- Consistent visual hierarchy

**Readability First**
- Font size minimum: 24px for body text, 36px+ for headlines
- High contrast between text and background (not grey text on light grey)
- Plenty of white space; don't cram content
- One focal point per slide (don't compete multiple graphics)

**Minimalist Design Approach**
- Remove anything that doesn't support the message
- If a graphic doesn't add clarity, delete it
- Data visualizations: Put one graph per slide, not graphs + text
- Decoration is fine, but supporting content comes first

**Color & Professionalism**
- Use a clean palette (2 base colors + 1 accent)
- Avoid rainbows, neon, or competing saturated colors
- Light backgrounds with dark text, or dark backgrounds with light text (high contrast)

### What NOT to Do

- Don't use generic stock photos that don't relate to your message
- Don't add animations or transitions (LinkedIn strips these out anyway)
- Don't make slides too dense with text
- Don't use all caps for long passages (headlines OK)
- Don't mix serif and sans-serif fonts randomly
- Don't change background colors randomly between slides

## Part 5: Pre-Publication Quality Check

### Content Validation

**Headline Quality**
- [ ] Slide 1 hook is specific, not generic ("5 LinkedIn tactics..." not "LinkedIn tips...")
- [ ] Each slide headline advances the story (not just labels)
- [ ] Slides 2-final: Each headline is scannable in 2 seconds
- [ ] Final slide CTA is clear and specific

**Text Density**
- [ ] No slide has more than 30 words of body text
- [ ] No slide requires more than 5 seconds to read
- [ ] Numbering used where appropriate (creates forward motion)
- [ ] Jargon explained or removed

**Structure**
- [ ] Narrative arc is clear (Problem→Insight→Action, Framework, etc.)
- [ ] One core idea per slide
- [ ] Final slide has a clear call-to-action
- [ ] Slide count: 5-10 (7 optimal)

**Design Consistency**
- [ ] Same fonts throughout (2 max)
- [ ] Same 3 color palette throughout
- [ ] Margins and spacing consistent
- [ ] Logo/branding in same place each slide
- [ ] No random background color changes

**Mobile Readiness**
- [ ] Text at least 24px minimum
- [ ] High contrast between text and background
- [ ] Portrait orientation used (1080×1350)
- [ ] No essential content in corners (safe zone for mobile)

### Engagement Optimization

**First Slide (Most Important)**
- [ ] Hook is bold, surprising, or asks a question
- [ ] Value prop is crystal clear ("You'll learn X")
- [ ] Minimal text (10 words or fewer for main message)
- [ ] High contrast, signals quality content
- [ ] Includes swipe indicator (arrow, text, etc.)

**Pacing & Flow**
- [ ] Slides 2-6 maintain consistent depth of content
- [ ] No slide feels isolated or disconnected
- [ ] Numbered slides build momentum
- [ ] Content gets progressively more actionable toward end

**Save-Ability** (Critical for algorithm)
- [ ] Someone could save this carousel to reference later
- [ ] Frameworks are named and memorable ("The 3-C Model")
- [ ] Checklists are complete and actionable
- [ ] Insights are generalizable, not one-off observations

## Part 6: LinkedIn Posting

### Post Caption Formula

Keep captions short. Let the carousel do the work.

**Structure:**
1. Hook (one line): Frames the problem or promise
2. Context (one line): One sentence about relevance
3. CTA (implied): Let the carousel deliver the value

**Example:**
"I see founders making the same 5 mistakes with their GTM. Swipe through to see which ones are costing you revenue 👇"

**Caption Best Practices:**
- [ ] Starts with a hook (question, stat, or promise)
- [ ] No more than 3-4 lines
- [ ] One clear call-to-action (swipe, comment, save)
- [ ] Matches the tone of your carousel
- [ ] No hashtag spam (LinkedIn deprioritizes over-hashtagged posts)

### Upload Steps

1. Click **"Start a post"** at the top of your LinkedIn feed
2. Click the **add icon** (+ or paperclip)
3. Select **"Document"** to upload your PDF
4. Upload your PDF file
5. LinkedIn will show a preview of your slides
6. Add a **document title** (e.g., "5 GTM Mistakes Founders Make")
   - This appears above the carousel
   - Keep it clear and specific
7. Write your caption (see formula above)
8. Click **"Post"**

### Timing & Distribution

**Best Times to Post** (2026 data):
- Tuesday or Wednesday: Highest engagement
- 10 AM-12 PM local time: Peak visibility
- Avoid: Friday afternoons, weekends, Monday mornings

**After Publishing:**
- [ ] Monitor first 30 minutes for engagement
- [ ] If slow start, comment on your own post with additional insight
- [ ] Respond to comments within first hour
- [ ] Share to other channels (email, Slack) if relevant

## Part 7: Performance Tracking

### Metrics to Monitor

**High-Value Signals** (in order of importance):
1. **Saves** (5x more important than likes): Are people saving to reference later?
2. **Dwell Time** (15-20 sec target): Are people spending time swiping?
3. **Comments**: Are people engaging in discussion?
4. **Shares & Reposts**: Is it shareable/quotable?
5. **Clicks** (if you included links): Did people follow through?

**Benchmarks** (2026 data):
- Average engagement rate: 6.60% (carousels vs 0.8% static images)
- Ideal dwell time: 15-20 seconds
- Reach multiplier: Carousels get 3.4x more reach than single-image posts
- Save rate: 5-10% of engaged viewers (high intent)

### What to Iterate On

If engagement is low:
- Was the hook weak? (Test a stronger first slide)
- Did the narrative lose people mid-carousel? (Cut slides 2-5, reorder)
- Was it too long? (Carousels over 12 slides see significant drop-off)
- Was the CTA unclear? (Make final slide action crystal clear)

If engagement is high:
- Identify which slides got the most saves/comments
- Notice which content type resonates (frameworks, narratives, data, contrarian takes)
- Reuse similar structures in future carousels

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Text Walls**
- Don't put paragraphs on slides
- Rule: If it takes more than 5 seconds to read, delete half of it

**Mistake 2: Too Many Slides**
- Carousels over 12 slides see engagement drop-off
- Ideal: 7 slides
- Acceptable: 5-10 slides

**Mistake 3: Weak Hook**
- First slide determines 80% of performance
- "LinkedIn tips" doesn't work; "5 tactics that generated 500 leads" does

**Mistake 4: Generic Advice**
- "Consistency matters" is forgettable
- "Posting on Tuesday-Wednesday at 10 AM increases engagement by 34%" is saveable

**Mistake 5: No Clear CTA**
- Don't end with "Thanks for reading"
- End with: "Comment which mistake you made" or "Save this for later" or "Follow for more"

**Mistake 6: Inconsistent Design**
- Changing fonts/colors randomly looks amateur
- Use same 2 fonts and 3 colors throughout

**Mistake 7: Optimizing for Wrong Metrics**
- Likes are less important than saves
- Comments are less important than long, substantive comments
- Focus on dwell time (15-20 seconds) and saves (people bookmarking)

## Required: Independent Evaluation (Run After Every Session)

**Do not grade your own work.** The agent that wrote the carousel is the worst judge of it — it knows what it *meant* and will quietly give itself credit for things that aren't on the page. To follow LLM-as-judge best practice, scoring is done by a **separate evaluator agent** that only sees the brief and the output.

After delivering a carousel (or carousel guidance), **always** dispatch it to the independent evaluator without being asked, then relay the verdict and act on it.

### How to dispatch

Spawn the **`carousel-evaluator`** subagent (via the Agent tool, `subagent_type: "carousel-evaluator"`). Pass it two things and nothing else that could bias it:

1. **The original user brief** plus any discovery answers the user gave.
2. **The full carousel you produced** (every slide's headline + body, the caption, and any surrounding guidance).

Do **not** pass your own reasoning, justifications, or a suggested score. The evaluator reads the authoritative rubric (`linkedin_carousel_eval/grading-rubric.json`), scores blind, and returns a scorecard with a pass/fail verdict and specific gaps. Relay its scorecard to the user verbatim — do not edit, soften, or re-score it yourself.

### What you'll be judged on (so write to this — but never grade it yourself)

Content is 75% of the score, silent craft + discovery is 25%:

- **Topical Substance & Specificity (25%)** — real, save-worthy content on the user's topic, not generic filler
- **Hook & Narrative Quality (20%)** — swipe-earning hook tied to the topic; arc to a payoff
- **Personalization & Voice (20%)** — built from the user's audience, expertise, story, and voice
- **Slide-Ready Deliverable (10%)** — paste-able slide copy + caption, not advice about making one
- **Silent Craft Adherence (15%)** — format applied invisibly; **reciting engagement stats at the user is penalized**
- **Discovery & Fit (10%)** — discovery questions asked (or assumptions stated) before drafting

Pass threshold: **90%**, with every dimension ≥ 2.0. The full score levels and assertion tests live in `linkedin_carousel_eval/grading-rubric.json` — the evaluator's source of truth. You don't reproduce them here and you don't pre-judge; your job is to write well, dispatch honestly, and respond to the verdict.

### Retry Loop (Required) — driven by the evaluator, not by you

The evaluator decides pass/fail. You react:

**If the evaluator returns PASS (≥ 90%, all dimensions ≥ 2.0):**
→ Done. Relay its scorecard and stop.

**If it returns FAIL on Attempt 1:**
→ Relay the scorecard labeled `(Attempt 1 of 2)`.
→ Revise the carousel, targeting every gap and required fix the evaluator named.
→ **Re-dispatch the revised carousel to a fresh `carousel-evaluator`** (a new instance — do not argue with the first one or grade the fix yourself).
→ Relay the new scorecard labeled `(Attempt 2 of 2)`, then apply the rule below.

**If it returns FAIL on Attempt 2:**
→ Relay the scorecard labeled `(Attempt 2 of 2 — Manual Review Required)` and add:

```
⚠️ Manual Review Required
This carousel scored below 90% after two independent evaluations.
Gaps that still failed: [the dimensions/assertions the evaluator flagged]
Recommended action: review the brief with the user, or update SKILL.md /
the grading rubric if the standard itself is off.
```

→ Do NOT attempt a third revision. Stop and wait for user input.

**Rules:** Never grade your own output. Never overrule the evaluator's score. Each revision gets a fresh evaluator instance. Never run more than 2 revision cycles per session.

> Single source of truth: the rubric lives in `linkedin_carousel_eval/grading-rubric.json` and the judge's instructions in `.claude/agents/carousel-evaluator.md`. Update those, not a copy here, when the standard changes.
